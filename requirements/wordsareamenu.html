Words Are a Menu.
The World Is Not.

AI already predicts — that's all it does. The question is what it predicts next: today the next word, tomorrow the next moment. And the real gap isn't building these systems. It's governing them.

I went down a rabbit hole recently. It started with a question I couldn't shake: we went from machine learning, to chatbots, to generative AI, to agents — each step doing more on its own. What comes next?

I expected the answer to be a bigger chatbot. It wasn't. The deeper I pulled — through research manifestos, billion-dollar funding rounds, and footage of workers in Tamil Nadu wearing head-mounted cameras — the more everything pointed at one missing piece: machines that don't just talk about the world, but can imagine what it does next. The field calls them world models, and more than two billion dollars is currently betting on them. ◆ SignalSumming the disclosed raises named later in this piece; a floor, not an audited total. This piece retraces that rabbit hole in plain language, and ends where my corner of the world, risk management, turned out to matter more than I expected.

2It will likely arrive as a split ◇ inference: large models reasoning slowly in data centres, smaller fast copies reacting instantly on devices.

3The metrics for testing these systems exist in fragments. The governance does not exist anywhere. That — not the models — is the under-populated corner of the race.

What comes next is a machine that imagines

Start with your phone's keyboard. As you type, it suggests the next word. That, blown up to enormous scale, is a language model: a machine with one habit — predict what comes next in the text. A world model has a different habit. It predicts what comes next in the world — and crucially, what comes next if I do something.

situation + action → new situation
The whole idea in one line. The "situation" might be a chessboard, a road scene, or every balance in a bank's database. The word that changes everything is action.

Think of a chess player: "if I move here, the board becomes this; if they answer there, it becomes that." She plays the game forward in her head before touching a piece. That mental fast-forward is what lets a machine plan instead of react. You may have already met a narrow one on the road: a self-driving car doesn't stop at "that's a pedestrian" — it predicts "that pedestrian is about to step out — brake." Cars are where the trick escaped the lab first, because driving forced the issue. The race is to make it general.

One clarification worth making early, because sharp readers ask it: what about agents? An agent isn't another kind of model. It's a role: the thing that acts toward a goal. And a chatbot is not the opposite of "rolling forward" — feeding its own output back in is exactly how a chatbot writes. The real difference is what rolls forward. A language model rolls words off a fixed menu. A world model rolls the world forward, and what you do changes what comes next. Today's agents, lacking one, imagine consequences in sentences. Fine for booking a flight; hopeless wherever one consequence leads to the next, because a sentence is not a simulation.

Why it doesn't exist yet: the world has no menu

Language, to a model, is a menu. Every model has a fixed vocabulary of "tokens" — word-pieces — and the menu is small: GPT-2's had about 50,000 entries; modern models run around 100,000 or more. "Predict the next word" means picking one item off a known, finite menu whose entries already mean something. That is the quiet luck behind the entire chatbot era.

The world hands you no such menu. The next moment is millions of pixels — every leaf, every shadow — unknowable in detail and mostly irrelevant. Now the complication: much real world-model work builds a menu anyway. Some teams chop video into invented word-pieces. DeepMind's Genie goes further: shown mountains of video with no labels at all, it invents its own small set of action codes — nobody named them, they mean nothing outside the model, but they are a menu. ◆ SignalGenie (DeepMind): action codes inferred from video with no action labels — which also means the "video shows the scene move but never the hand" data wall is real but under active attack. So the sharp version of this essay's title claim: language comes with a menu whose entries already carry meaning; for the world, you must learn the menu — and deciding what its entries throw away is the unsolved part. Predict "the glass will shatter"; ignore where each shard lands. That choosing-what-to-ignore is the filter, and it is the heart of the race. Yann LeCun's design, JEPA, is the camp that refuses the menu entirely — predicting only the things that matter, in the model's own abstract terms, rather than in any vocabulary of the world.

Two walls stand behind the filter. Data: the web holds oceans of video showing the world changing, but rarely records the action — a wall the Genie line of work is chipping away at. Drift: chain a learned simulator's predictions, each output feeding the next input, and tiny errors pile up; ten steps out, it is confidently describing a world that does not exist. Remember that word, drift. This essay ends on it.

And note what the bottleneck is not: shrinking. Distillation — compressing a big model into a small one — has been routine since Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues wrote it up in 2015, and systems like AlphaZero already compress slow, careful planning into fast instinct in narrow games. The missing piece is a general master worth compressing — and it is blocked by the menu, the data, and drift.

The proof it can be done is a baby — inherited twice

A baby is the proof that the filter can be built. Her eyes take in a flood of raw detail — though a newborn's eyesight itself is blurry, roughly 20/400, sharpening over months; some of the flood is throttled before it ever reaches her, and that throttling is itself part of how the filter gets built ◇ inference. Even so, she isn't overwhelmed. When a ball rolls behind the couch and fails to come out the other side, she looks longer — the standard sign, in infant research, of a broken expectation. Notice what broke it: not a wrong pixel — a wrong event. She predicts at the level of things that matter. The filter looks to be built in from the start ◇ inference.

And she doesn't start from zero. She inherits twice. Genes give her the hardware and the drives — eyes wired for faces, reflexes, and above all the itch: to try, to predict, to be surprised, to poke. Culture gives her everything the grown-ups compressed over generations — ingredients that sit waiting, switched on by doing: infants learn language from live back-and-forth with a person, not from a recording playing in the room ◆ signal — which fits this essay's own argument better than passive viewing would: action, not observation, is what activates the inheritance. Here is the mapping that made the field click for me: the language model is the machine's cultural inheritance. The web is humanity's compressed experience, and pre-training swallowed it whole. What no one has built is the genetic half — the itch that makes a baby learn to sit, walk, and drop spoons with no teacher at all. One honest concession before leaning on her too hard: evolution is the outer loop that produced the itch, and training from scratch with reinforcement learning is precisely the attempt to re-run that loop. Not impossible — just brutally expensive. The baby proves the filter can exist; she doesn't prove there's a shortcut to it. ◇ InferenceWorth restating on its own: the existence proof and the shortcut are two different claims, and only the first one is solid.

The field, meanwhile, splits two ways — not the tidy duel it's often painted as. One split: start from language or start from video. Google's robot models ride a language-model backbone, while Genie learns from video alone. The other split: predict every pixel or predict only what matters — video generators paint whole frames; JEPA refuses to. LeCun left Meta in late 2025, disagreement over direction and a corporate reshuffle both in the mix, and in March 2026 raised a $1.03 billion seed round — the largest in European history — to bet on video-first, abstract-space. ◆ SignalWidely reported at the time in the tech and business press; figures as publicly disclosed at raise. Three weeks earlier, Fei-Fei Li's World Labs raised $1 billion on top of an earlier $230 million, and has shipped a product, Marble, which builds explorable 3D scenes. Overlapping bets, not the same one: Marble makes worlds you can look at; LeCun wants models that predict what happens when you act.

What could the itch actually be? Four serious candidates. Surprise as the teacher — this is how JEPA already learns — though a training signal tells the machine how to learn, not why to act, and that gap is the itch. Curiosity — a built-in pull toward whatever it can't yet predict, with a famous trap: a naïvely curious machine gets hypnotised by a TV tuned to static. A body — watching shows what follows what; only poking reveals what causes what, and the baby drops the spoon on purpose. And pre-wiring — a few starter assumptions, like "objects persist". My guess: no single one wins; the answer is the combination. Which is uncomfortably close to a description of a baby.

The body is the data collector — and the harvest has begun

The toddler's advantage wasn't only the flood of video — it was the senses, plural, and above all touch: vision tells you what things look like; touch tells you weight, grip, softness — the physics you cannot see. There's a reason babies put everything in their mouths.

A humanoid robot is the machine finally getting the full set of senses. It also answers the data wall directly, because a robot knows exactly what it just did and can feel what happened next. My reframe of the humanoid race: those companies aren't really selling mechanical labour yet. They're building harvesting machines for the loop this essay opened with: situation + action → new situation.

And the harvest has already begun, before the robots are ready, using humans as the temporary hardware: AFP reported this June on thousands of workers across southern India wearing head-mounted cameras, paid roughly $2.60 an hour to record themselves folding clothes and making coffee — first-person footage feeding what the industry calls Large Behaviour Models. ◆ SignalAFP, June 2026, widely syndicated. Meta runs a version of the same harvest through sensor-fitted research glasses. The web was humanity's verbal culture, compressed accidentally, for free — and the language model inherited it. Those recordings are humanity's physical culture, compressed deliberately, by the hour. The first inheritance was a byproduct of civilisation; the second is an industry — one being built faster than its rules, as the same reporting notes: workers teaching machines their own jobs, in at least one case without being meaningfully told what the footage was for.

The last clue is in your own reflexes

Try to tickle yourself. It doesn't work — your brain cancels the sensation, because it already predicted exactly what your own hand was about to do. A part of your brain, the cerebellum, is widely believed to run forward models of your own movements: forecasting the next fraction of a second of what your body will feel and pre-loading the response ◆ signal. That's the same shape as this whole essay's idea — situation plus action, predicted forward — just running on your own muscles instead of a chessboard. Not everything fast is prediction, though: yanking your hand off a stove is a hardwired shortcut with no prediction in it — evolution's pre-wiring, a live example of the genetic half machines lack. The one-line summary of the brain's trick, and of the machines' ambition ◇ inference: intelligence is prediction done in advance, not computation on demand.

Everyone is building the runner. Nobody owns the judge.

The race even has a finish line of sorts. In 2017, Google published the transformer paper; inside the field, language AI was rebuilt on it within two years, and the public moment came in 2020 with GPT-3. World models sit somewhere on that same lag: the name stuck in 2018 (Ha and Schmidhuber — the idea runs back to the early 1990s), the manifesto arrived in 2022 (LeCun), and the paper that cracks the learned menu at scale hasn't happened — arguably◇ InferenceGenie is the strongest counter-case to this sentence: whether it counts as "cracking" the menu at meaningful scale, or as a narrower proof of concept, is a live disagreement, not a settled one. — though a sceptical reader will point at Genie and ask why it doesn't count. The billions are racing toward a paper, not a product. Which makes the other gap harder to ignore.

Here is that gap — stated carefully, because my first draft claimed too much.

A learned world model is a simulator built from data instead of equations, and its characteristic failure is drift: error that piles up while the confidence stays high. The field is not blind to this. One research line (Dreamer) has measured how error grows with each step predicted ahead; a separate line of work (Janner and colleagues, on model-based reinforcement learning) showed why that compounding error is exactly the reason researchers keep imagined rollouts short; the self-driving world runs its own simulations and writes formal safety cases ◆ signal. The alarm bells exist. The measurements exist — in fragments, scattered across papers, each lab grading its own homework.

What's still missing — for this kind of model, learned and general-purpose and conditioned on your actions — is banking's specific mechanism: backtesting with exceptions counted against thresholds fixed in advance, calibration and sharpness scored so a model can't cheat by hedging, and reporting that makes it someone's job. Narrower cases already have pieces of the broader instinct: flight simulators take an action and predict a consequence, but they're physics, not learned from data; weather models are learned, but nothing you do changes the forecast; and this year a handful of researchers have started reaching for the same institutional idea from a different regulated discipline, importing aerospace and automotive safety-validation practice instead of banking's. ◆ SignalOefinger, Schäfer, Moller, Piccinini & Betz, RSS 2026 Workshop on Robot World Models (July 2026) — an analogous transplant, different mechanism, same instinct, arriving the same summer. That's the specific gap: not that nobody's reaching for the idea, but that nobody's brought banking's own version of it — backtesting, thresholds, the calibration-and-sharpness pair — to bear here.

Banking built exactly this discipline — after confident models lost real money. Independent validation teams that do not report to the model builders. Backtesting: yesterday's stated confidence checked against today's outcome, with the misses — "exceptions" — counted against thresholds that trigger action automatically. Regulatory reporting that makes all of it someone's job. For lay readers: backtesting is how banks check whether a risk model's confidence was honest, after the fact. It is the closest working example on earth of what world models will need before anyone bets lives or capital on a machine's imagined future. So the contribution I'm interested in is not inventing a metric. It's importing the governance.

And I'm going to build the smallest honest version of it — specified here, so you can hold me to it.

The worldsA foxes-and-rabbits system (the Lotka–Volterra equations): populations that rise and fall in a known, steady cycle, so the true answer is never in doubt. And a double pendulum, because chaos forces the hardest question. Each world has a lever — removing rabbits, pushing the pivot — and every prediction the judge grades is "what happens if I do this," never just "what happens next." One trap dodged from the start: the true world and the learned model get the same maths engine and the same time-step — otherwise every chart measures the engine, not the model.

The hardest questionFar enough ahead, even a perfect model loses the true path, because tiny differences in the starting point blow up. Grading against zero error would "prove" a flawless model untrustworthy. So the judge must separate the model's mistakes from the world's own unpredictability: compare the model's drift against how fast the real system drifts away from itself, and past that point, grade the overall pattern rather than the exact path — the weather-versus-climate distinction. That is the difference between an honest simulator and a confident dream, stated precisely.

Measured & publishedOne-step accuracy against two deliberately dumb baselines — "nothing changes" and "carry on in a straight line" — because without a baseline, a small error means nothing. Error as the model predicts further ahead, compared against how fast the world itself becomes unpredictable. An honesty check: when the model says it is 90% sure, is it right about 90% of the time — including from starting points it never saw in training, because "does it know when it doesn't know" is the money question. A separate check that it isn't just hedging: "somewhere between zero and a million" is always right and always useless, so a vague model doesn't get to win on honesty alone. And a defined trust horizon: how many steps ahead the model can be believed, for a named task.

Presented asBacktesting exception plots: every time reality lands outside what the model said was likely, circled and counted against a limit. Readers from the risk world will recognise the chart on sight. Everyone else now has the one sentence.

AI has always been a prediction machine — the revolution is moving it from predicting the next word to predicting the next moment. Two pieces are missing, and they are not the same kind of missing. The itch — whatever makes a baby learn the world with no teacher — is a billion-dollar race, and I'll watch it from the stands. The judge — the governance that decides when a learned simulator may be trusted — has no owner, no budget, and no name in this race. Banking has run that discipline for decades. I intend to show, at toy scale, what it looks like applied. Drift curves and all.
